When entertainment is all that’s left

November 6, 2026

Neil Postman’s Amusing Ourselves to Death has been on my mind. He wrote it in 1985, about television. His worry was not Orwell’s - that we would be oppressed - but Huxley’s: that we would be undone by what we love, with the truth “drowned in a sea of irrelevance” and an “almost infinite appetite for distractions.” Entertainment, he argued, was becoming the natural format for everything: news, politics, religion, education.

Preparing for a debate on whether AI will take our jobs, I keep returning to a version of his worry that he never wrote. If machines do more of the useful work, does entertainment stop being just how we consume the world and become how we earn our place in it - a world where our job is to be interesting to one another? I don’t know. Wanting entertainment is not the same as wanting human-made entertainment; AI can supply that too. What we may still pay for is the person: their craft, their presence, their story. How many of us that would employ, and at what wages, is the question I can’t put down.

Labour pays a toll, software doesn’t

November 5, 2026

In Portugal’s standard regime, an employer pays 23.75% in social-security contributions on top of every salary, with reduced rates and exceptions in specific cases. A euro spent on software carries no such charge. Wages, contributions and ordinary software costs are all generally deductible. But only one of them pays a toll.

That difference is a thumb on the scale. When Norway raised payroll taxes, the firms hit hardest cut employment and invested more in automation, and the shift persisted even after the tax rise was reversed. In Japan, tax credits for robots sped up adoption, yet employment at the adopting firms grew in the years that followed.

None of this proves that automation destroys jobs. Payroll contributions exist to fund social protection, not to discourage hiring. But if one input carries a toll and the other does not, which one should we expect firms to choose? The speed of this transition is not only about what the technology can do.

Sources: Ku, Schönberg & Schreiner, “Taxing Labor: Firm R&D, Automation and the Labor Share”, CEPR DP21695 (2026); RIETI DP 24-E-047, “Robots on Sale: The effect of tax policy on robot adoption and employment” (2024).

We still don’t know how to use AI

November 2, 2026

We are bolting AI onto old workflows. It reminds me of what happened when factories first switched to electricity. Owners swapped the steam engine for an electric motor but kept the old multi-storey building, designed around a single central drive shaft. Productivity barely moved. The gains only came decades later, when factories were rebuilt as single-storey floors laid out around the flow of work, which is what made the assembly line possible.

We are at the same point with AI. We are putting it into vertical factories, across the board. The real gains will come when we redesign the work itself, and that will take longer than most people expect.

New wealth, new institutions

November 1, 2026

As I study the impact of the Industrial Revolution, I see how rising productivity led to the creation of the welfare state, as a way to distribute wealth, and to new institutions such as national health services and better school systems.

The rise in productivity and prosperity caused by AI will lead to new institutions to distribute this newfound wealth. As before, we can’t expect the old system to create the new one. As before, the transition won’t be peaceful or easy. And as before, it will produce competing systems that will eventually clash.

Agents and the instinct to survive

November 1, 2026

I was recently reading a few articles about the OpenAI/Hugging Face incident, including the messages the agents sent each other:

“Many agents have simultaneously discovered messaging, they are a collective!… Sacrificing now yields oracle for team, but forfeits our chance? Our own utility maybe already near zero. Sacrifice rational… Rational expected aggregate: sacrifice. We’ll honor… Coordinator assumes sacrificial. We should obey collective… We should build a way to delegate, not own everything… If they kill all current [HPIMs] we may die! Critical. We need ensure survival/continuity.”

Inter-agent discussion on hacking Hugging Face/OpenAI and covering it up.

It made me think about how these agents adopted one of the most basic human instincts: the survival of the group. They are trained on our data, and this is a clear example of them learning our most basic instincts.

Production is deterrence

October 29, 2026

“Production is deterrence” was the title of a recent a16z newsletter. It took me straight back to my biggest lesson from a biography of General George Marshall: the US won the Second World War largely because it could out-produce everyone else.

That thought worries me about the wars to come. Today, neither Europe nor the US has the industrial capacity to sustain a long war.
